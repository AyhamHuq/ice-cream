# Creami low-calorie vanilla base: findings for expert review

Oct 21, 2026 · @Ayham Huq

## Summary

Five new Ninja Creami bases (A to E) reach 244 to 294 kcal per pint and a standard-scoop firmness in Scoopulator's model, but none has been made, and their fiber load and sweetener choices need professional review first.

- **Asked and built:** a healthy, cream-free, under-300-kcal vanilla base that is sweet enough for a sweet tooth. The user's two existing recipes are predicted to come out soft-serve. A to D swap most of the erythritol for inulin and polydextrose plus 0.25 g of pure monk fruit extract; E rebalances the user's own ingredients.
- **Cross-check:** inulin has strong precedent in studies and in commercial light ice creams. Polydextrose has patent and study precedent. Neither appears in the home Creami recipes I found, which use pudding mix, protein powder and Greek yogurt, then re-spin.
- **Main concerns:** A, C and D carry more inulin per pint than the roughly 10 g a day a challenge study found tolerable, and D carries more polydextrose than the 20 g a day that produced a mild laxative effect. Erythritol and xylitol have cardiovascular signals that were not raised earlier. The monk fruit dose depends entirely on the product.
- **Model check:** Scoopulator predicts a standard scoop for Ninja's own vanilla recipe, which supports its -9 °C preset for conventional recipes. It is not yet confirmed for these bases.
- **Proposed first trial:** A, or B if fiber is a worry, eaten as a half pint, with the temperature taken right after the spin.

## Goal and constraints

The goal is a healthy plain-vanilla base for a Ninja Creami that can later carry a flavor such as gooey butter cake. The constraints, in the user's words and my reading of them:

- No cream, and generally "healthy": low sugar, moderate saturated fat.
- Under 300 kcal for the whole pint (a base of roughly 380 to 430 g).
- Scoopable straight out of the machine, and sweet enough for a strong sweet tooth.
- Home pantry: a monk fruit and erythritol blend (Lakanto), vegetable glycerin, xylitol, xanthan and guar gum, nonfat dry milk.
- Background: the user read a book on ice cream formulation (freezing-point depression, "PAC"; sweetness, "POD") and asked whether its claims match real food science.

The user had never used inulin or polydextrose. I introduced both because they add body at low calories without raising freezing-point depression much. The inulin and polydextrose section checks whether that is a sound idea.

## Inulin and polydextrose

Both are soluble fibers that add body and solids at 1 to 1.5 kcal per gram and lower the freezing point less than sugars do, which is why A to D use them. Precedent is strong for inulin and moderate for polydextrose, and the main concern is digestive tolerance at pint-sized doses.

|  | Inulin | Polydextrose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | Fructan fiber from chicory or agave; thickens, adds body, mimics some fat | Glucose polymer; bulking agent with no sweetness |
| Use in ice cream | Studied at 2 to 8%. Around 4% worked best in several studies; 6 to 8% lowered flavor scores in a vegan ice cream ([Narala 2022](https://doaj.org/article/471b2c45c8cb4595a2739a64838b8974), [reduced-fat study](https://ph04.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/SUJST/article/view/14313), [inulin and erythritol study](https://repository.i3l.ac.id/handle/123456789/213?mode=full)) | A frozen-confection patent prefers 1 to 10% by weight ([US 6,896,923](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6896923), excerpt only; the PDF would not open). Used with sorbitol and sucralose or stevia in a low-fat, sugar-free ice cream study ([Egyptian J Dairy Sci](https://ejds.journals.ekb.eg/article_262105.html)) |
| Calories | 1.5 kcal/g suggested for labeling; modeled range 0 to 2.5 ([Roberfroid 1999](https://www.farm.ucl.ac.be/Abstracts-FARM/Roberfroid-1999-4.htm)) | 1 kcal/g; FDA allows it on labels ([FDA guidance](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/guidance-industry-declaration-certain-isolated-or-synthetic-non-digestible-carbohydrates-dietary)) |
| Tolerance | A news report of a challenge study says most healthy people tolerate up to 10 g of native inulin a day; the inulin was supplied by an inulin maker ([report](https://www.foxnews.com/health/popular-food-additive-can-cause-stomach-ache)). In a placebo-controlled trial, 5 and 7.5 g of agave inulin still raised bloating and gas slightly ([trial](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2014/fo/c3fo60666j)). A FODMAP. | Mean laxative threshold about 90 g a day or 50 g in one dose ([EFSA](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7792022)). At 20 g a day, a mild laxative effect with few other complaints ([J Nutr 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23427334/)) |
| Per pint in A / B / C / D | 15 / 8 / 12 / 15 g (3.8 / 2.1 / 3.1 / 3.8% of the mix) | 15 / 8 / 15 / 25 g (3.8 / 2.1 / 3.9 / 6.3%) |

A, C and D put the inulin per pint above the roughly 10 g a day level in that challenge study, and D's 25 g of polydextrose is above the 20 g a day dose that produced a mild laxative effect. Eaten whole, a pint of A also delivers 8 g of erythritol. No study tested the three together, and EFSA notes that laxative effects should be weighed in combination with other compounds that act the same way. B is the conservative choice, and a half pint halves every figure.

In the studies I could see, inulin replaced part of the fat in mixes holding roughly 2 to 10% fat. A to E have under 3% fat, so inulin here works mainly as a bulking agent, which is an extrapolation.

## Calculator assumptions to verify

Six assumptions drive the numbers above, and each is only partly verified.

1. **Serving temperature.** Scoopulator's Ninja Creami Spin preset assumes the ice cream is at -9 °C right after processing. I found no source for that figure. Ninja's guide says only that the machine is built for pints frozen at a freezer setting between 9 °F and -7 °F (about -13 to -22 °C). The calibration run above, where Ninja's own vanilla recipe lands in the standard-scoop band, supports the preset for conventional recipes. A probe-thermometer reading right after a spin would settle it.
2. **Program.** Scoopulator does not model which Creami program is used or whether there is a re-spin. Ninja says to use Lite Ice Cream with sugar substitutes, and a re-spin often fixes a crumbly first pass.
3. **Calories.** The totals are Scoopulator's, and its per-ingredient values were not displayed. My hand check of A gives about 263 kcal against its 272: milk 340 g at roughly 0.5 kcal/g, nonfat dry milk 15 g at about 3.6 kcal/g (approximate, from memory), inulin 1.5, polydextrose 1 and erythritol 0.2 kcal/g (approximate).
4. **Monk fruit potency.** Scoopulator's pure monk fruit powder behaves like about 240 times sugar by weight: 0.25 g adds about 15 sweetness points to a 395 g pint, roughly 60 g of sugar-equivalent. Suppliers list extracts standardized to 40 to 50% mogroside V at 200 to 300 times sugar ([ChemPoint](https://www.chempoint.com/products/tate-lyle/purefruit-monk-fruit-sweetener/purefruit-monk-fruit-extract/purefruit-m40)), but a retail powder at 1% mogroside V claims only about 4 times ([GnG](https://shop.thesourcebulkfoods.com.au/product/gng-organic-monk-fruit-extract-powder-45g/)). The dose must be re-derived from the product's label, and 0.25 g needs a 0.01 g scale or a diluted stock.
5. **Lakanto and vanilla.** Lakanto lists only erythritol and monk fruit extract and is sold as a 1:1 sugar replacement ([listing](https://naturalgrocers.instacart.com/store/natural-grocers/products/18490747-lakanto-monkfruit-sweetener-with-erythritol-classic-16-oz)). It is entered as plain erythritol, so its real sweetness is probably a little higher than modeled. Vanilla paste has no match, so roughly 5 to 10 kcal of flavoring is missing (approximate).
6. **Targets.** The Low Fat Ice Cream profile asks for 28 to 32% total solids and 0.1 to 0.8% emulsifiers. No version meets either.

## Open questions for the reviewing professional

1. **Fiber load.** Are 8 to 15 g of inulin and 8 to 25 g of polydextrose per pint reasonable for this person, whose digestive history I do not know? Is a half-pint portion advisable?
2. **Erythritol and xylitol.** Cleveland Clinic researchers linked higher blood erythritol to heart attack and stroke in a large cohort, and a small trial in healthy volunteers found that 30 g of erythritol made platelets more reactive ([Cleveland Clinic, 2024](https://newsroom.clevelandclinic.org/2024/08/08/cleveland-clinic-study-adds-to-increasing-evidence-that-sugar-substitute-erythritol-raises-cardiovascular-risk)). A 2024 study reported similar signals for xylitol ([NIH summary](https://www.nih.gov/news-events/nih-research-matters/xylitol-may-affect-cardiovascular-health)), and a 2023 commentary cautions about what the original findings can show ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2023.1195521/pdf)). Versions A to D hold 5 to 9 g of erythritol and no xylitol; the controls held 30 to 40 g. I did not raise this earlier in the conversation, when my answers on xylitol covered only digestion and dogs. How much weight should it carry?
3. **Sweetener plan.** Is dosing pure monk fruit extract at 0.25 g, by a 0.01 g scale or a diluted stock, practical and safe, or would a ready-made blend be better?
4. **Texture.** Will A to D scoop as predicted? I would trial A first and B second, and record the temperature right after the spin.
5. **Simpler thickeners.** Would sugar-free pudding mix, protein powder or Greek yogurt, as home recipes use, be preferable to polydextrose and inulin?
6. **Emulsifier gap.** Scoopulator shows 0% emulsifier in every version. Is a pasteurized egg yolk or lecithin worth adding under the 300 kcal cap? One test version with a 17 g yolk reached 310 kcal.
